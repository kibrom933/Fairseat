The EU AI Act, if you only use AI tools

You run a small business. You did not build an AI system; you subscribe to a few. This is what the Act asks of you, with the dates as they stand after the Digital Omnibus on AI was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026.

General information, not legal advice. We link the sources at the bottom; where they disagree or are unclear, we say so.

You are a "deployer"

The Act calls a business that uses an AI system under its own authority a deployer. The company that makes the tool is the provider and carries most of the obligations. Personal, non-professional use is outside the Act.

The dates

FromWhatDoes it touch a small deployer?
2 Feb 2025Prohibited practices (Art. 5) and AI literacy (Art. 4)Yes. See below.
2 Aug 2025Rules for general-purpose AI model providersNo. This is for model makers.
2 Aug 2026Transparency duties (Art. 50) in generalYes, if you publish realistic AI-made media or run a chatbot.
2 Dec 2026Machine-readable marking of AI output (Art. 50(2)) for systems already on the market; new ban on AI that generates non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse materialMostly a provider duty. The ban applies to everyone.
2 Dec 2027High-risk systems listed in Annex III (was 2 Aug 2026)Only if you use AI for things like hiring, credit or access to education.
2 Aug 2028High-risk AI inside regulated products, Annex I (was 2 Aug 2027)Rarely.

What you need to do now

1. Help your people understand the tools

Article 4 has applied since February 2025. The Omnibus softened it: you must take measures to support the development of AI literacy among the people using AI for you, and the text now says this does not mean guaranteeing a particular level for any individual. For a five-person team, a written one-page note is a reasonable measure: which tools are approved, what must never be pasted into them, and that output is checked by a human before it goes to a client.

2. Stay away from the banned uses

The ones a small business could stumble into: inferring employees' emotions at work, and manipulative or exploitative techniques that cause harm. From 2 December 2026, tools that produce non-consensual intimate imagery are banned as well.

3. Label what needs a label

Since 2 August 2026, if you publish image, audio or video that is AI-generated or manipulated and could pass for real (a "deep fake"), you must disclose that it is artificial. Text published to inform the public on matters of public interest needs the same disclosure unless a human has reviewed it and someone holds editorial responsibility. If customers talk to a chatbot on your site, they must be told it is an AI unless that is obvious; making that possible is the provider's job, switching it on is yours. The Commission published guidelines on these duties on 20 July 2026.

4. Check whether anything you do is "high-risk"

Screening job applicants, evaluating employees, deciding on credit, or grading students with AI falls under Annex III. Those rules now start on 2 December 2027. If that is you, the duties are real (human oversight, following the provider's instructions, keeping logs, informing the people affected) and you should take advice well before then.

GDPR is still the bigger job

None of this replaces data protection law. For an ordinary meeting-notes or writing tool, the questions that matter day to day are where data is processed, whether it trains a model, how long it is kept, and whether you have a data processing agreement. The vendor check walks through them.

A one-page policy you can adapt

  • We use these AI tools for work: [list]. Other tools need a yes from [name] first.
  • We do not paste client personal data, passwords or contracts into a tool that is not on the list.
  • A person reads and takes responsibility for anything an AI tool wrote before it leaves the company.
  • Realistic AI-made images, audio or video are labelled as AI-generated when published.
  • We do not use AI to assess job applicants or staff.
  • Reviewed every six months by [name]. Last review: [date].
